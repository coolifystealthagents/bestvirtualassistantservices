# How to Evaluate a Virtual Assistant for Property Management Communications

Published 2026-10-05.

Property-management communication looks like an inbox task until a message reports a gas smell, requests an accommodation, disputes a charge, or alleges unequal treatment. A buyer should evaluate a virtual assistant service against those moments, not merely against response speed. The useful role is often to identify the property and resident, preserve the original message, apply an approved category, send a limited acknowledgement, and route the matter to the accountable manager. This guide helps a property manager design and test that service without handing policy decisions to an administrative role.

## How to Evaluate a Virtual Assistant for Property Management Communications: Map the message types before discussing staffing

Export a representative, privacy-protected sample of recent contacts and classify them by the decision they require. Separate routine status requests, maintenance intake, leasing questions, payment disputes, accommodation requests, complaints, and possible emergencies. Note channel, volume by hour, language needs, and the person who currently resolves each class. This prevents a provider from pricing a single generic inbox while the business quietly expects several different workflows. It also exposes questions the buyer must settle, such as whether after-hours coverage means acknowledgement, dispatch, or an on-call manager response.

## Write an emergency route that does not depend on interpretation

Define observable triggers and the exact route for fire, flooding, gas odor, loss of essential service, threats, or another condition selected by the property owner and qualified advisers. The assistant should not diagnose severity from incomplete facts. The procedure should preserve the resident's words, capture the property and callback details, deliver the approved emergency instruction, and notify the named responder through a tested channel. Test unanswered calls and failed notifications. A colorful priority label is not protection if nobody owns the alert after it is applied.

## Keep housing decisions with authorized people

Screening outcomes, lease interpretations, accommodation decisions, concessions, enforcement, and statements about legal rights should remain with trained, authorized owners unless the business has deliberately structured otherwise. The assistant can collect required information, identify missing fields, schedule an approved next step, and maintain the record. Ask the provider how scripts are controlled and how staff stop when a request falls outside them. HUD's fair-housing material is useful background, but the property manager should obtain qualified advice for its actual obligations and local rules.

## Design the maintenance handoff around a complete ticket

A usable ticket names the property and unit, reports the issue in the resident's own terms, records permission-to-enter information according to policy, lists availability, attaches supplied media safely, and shows prior related tickets. It must not turn an assistant's guess into a technical diagnosis. Decide which approved vendors may receive which details and who authorizes spend or entry. Review several tickets end to end with the maintenance owner; fast intake has little value if technicians must call back for basic facts or receive information they should not see.

## Score the service on safe resolution, not message count

Measure complete routing, correct use of approved acknowledgements, missed escalation triggers, duplicate tickets, resident follow-up caused by missing data, and manager review time. Keep waiting-on-resident and waiting-on-owner intervals separate from assistant handling. Sample across message types rather than reviewing only easy requests. A high number of closed conversations can conceal premature closure, while a modest throughput number may reflect careful handling of complex cases. Agree in advance what counts as a material error and what response follows one.

## Run a pilot with ordinary and difficult cases

Use fictional or properly protected cases that include a routine update, an incomplete maintenance report, a charge dispute, an accommodation request, and an urgent condition. Observe source use, record quality, escalation, and the handoff at shift end. Then release a bounded live queue with full review. The acceptance record should list the approved message classes, hours, scripts, property systems, escalation contacts, excluded decisions, and evidence from the pilot. Expansion should follow demonstrated control, not confidence gained during a sales call.

## A realistic boundary test

A resident writes, 'The ceiling is dripping again and the light is flickering.' The assistant links the message to the correct unit, preserves the wording and photo, applies the preapproved water-and-electrical trigger, and contacts the on-call property manager. The assistant does not promise a repair time or tell the resident the unit is safe. When the primary manager does not acknowledge the alert, the tested fallback route activates. The record shows each attempt and the approved resident instruction. During review, the owner can see whether the routing worked without asking the assistant to make a building-safety judgment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the assistant answer leasing questions?

Yes, when answers come from current, property-approved information and the process routes eligibility, exception, and policy questions to an authorized person.

### Should the provider receive access to every property record?

No. Start with the least access required for the approved queue and test how access is changed or removed.

### Is a fast first response the main KPI?

It is one measure. Correct routing, complete records, safe escalation, and avoided repeat contacts are equally important.
