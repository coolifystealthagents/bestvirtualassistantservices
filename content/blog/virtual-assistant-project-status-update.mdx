# Virtual Assistant Project Status Updates

A project status update is a narrative about change. It explains where the work stood at the start of a reporting window, what moved, what did not move, and what that means for the next decision. A virtual assistant can turn source notes into that narrative by staying close to evidence and labeling confidence instead of filling gaps with optimism.

## Virtual Assistant Project Status Updates: Establish the reporting window

Open with the period covered, the baseline, and the current position. The baseline might be the approved scope, the last accepted milestone, or the previous status update. State which one you used. Without a baseline, a list of completed tasks gives the reader no way to judge change.

The reporting window also protects against accidental mixing. A message received today may concern work from last week, while a file completed yesterday may not yet be reviewed. Note the cut-off used for the update and identify material evidence that arrived afterward. This makes the next update easier to compare.

## Tell the story of the delta

Organize the body around the difference from the last report. Name completed deliverables and the evidence that shows acceptance. Then describe current work that is underway, including what remains before it can be used or reviewed. Avoid treating meetings, drafts, and messages as outcomes unless they produced the agreed result.

A useful sequence is baseline, completed change, current change, and next change. It reads naturally because it follows the project’s movement. Keep background brief unless it explains a change in scope, forecast, dependency, or risk.

## Separate dependencies, risks, and issues

A dependency is work or information needed from another person or team. A risk is a possible future event that could affect the plan. An issue is an event that has already happened and needs action. These categories require different language.

For a dependency, name the required input, owner, and needed date. For a risk, describe the trigger, possible effect, current response, and confidence in the forecast. For an issue, state the observed impact, containment, decision owner, and next checkpoint. Do not call an unresolved issue a risk merely because the label sounds less urgent.

## Explain forecast confidence

Forecast confidence should reflect evidence, not mood. A simple rubric can help:

| Confidence | Evidence pattern | Wording to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High | Remaining work is understood, dependencies are confirmed, and recent progress matches the plan | “Forecast remains high confidence because…” |
| Medium | The path is clear, but one material assumption or dependency is open | “Forecast is medium confidence pending…” |
| Low | Scope, acceptance, or a critical dependency is unresolved | “Forecast is low confidence until…” |

The rubric does not predict the future. It explains why the current forecast deserves its label. If the confidence changes, identify the event that caused the change. A new dependency, a rejected deliverable, or an approved scope change is more informative than “confidence decreased.”

## Convert an activity dump into an update

Imagine the source notes say: “Held planning call. Reviewed three files. Sent questions. Updated tracker. Waiting for access. Drafted next steps.” That list records motion but not position.

An executive update could say: “During the August 10 to August 12 window, the team moved from initial review to a defined next-step plan. Three files were reviewed and the open questions were sent to the project owner. The tracker now reflects the agreed sequence. Work is currently paused on the next deliverable because access has not yet been provided. Forecast confidence is medium until access is confirmed. Decision needed: the access owner should confirm delivery by August 15, or approve a revised review sequence.”

The revised version keeps the evidence, adds a baseline and delta, distinguishes completed work from a dependency, and asks for a dated decision. It does not claim the project is healthy or delayed beyond what the notes support.

## Make decisions easy to answer

A decision request should state the choice, context, owner, and response date. If there are options, describe the consequence of each without disguising a preference as a fact. “Please confirm whether to wait for access or proceed with the approved offline review by Friday” is answerable. “Any thoughts?” is not.

Record decisions in the next update and explain their effect on scope, sequence, or forecast. If no response arrives, keep the request visible and update the consequence of waiting. Do not mark a decision complete because a reminder was sent.

When a decision has several participants, name the person who can authorize the next move rather than listing every recipient. Include the information that person needs, such as the affected deliverable, the available alternatives, and the date that keeps the current forecast credible. If the request is informational rather than an approval, label it that way. Readers should not have to infer whether a reply is optional.

Also note what will happen if the decision is late. The consequence may be a sequence change, a smaller review window, or a new forecast date. State that consequence as a planning effect, not as a threat. This gives the owner a fair chance to choose with the relevant context.

## Keep a useful history

Preserve the prior status and show what changed since it. A short change log can identify a completed deliverable, newly opened dependency, forecast movement, or scope decision. Avoid copying the entire previous update into every edition. Repetition makes the real delta harder to find.

Use the same labels for status, confidence, dependency, risk, issue, and decision request. Consistency helps a reader scan several projects while the narrative explains the particular facts. The [Project Management Institute communication research](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/project-communications-management-communication-9097) provides useful context on matching communication to audience and purpose.

## Finish with an accountable handoff

Before sending, verify the reporting window, baseline, completed deliverables, current work, dependencies, forecast confidence, risks, issues, decisions needed, owner, due date, and change since the last update. Link the evidence when appropriate and flag anything not verified.
