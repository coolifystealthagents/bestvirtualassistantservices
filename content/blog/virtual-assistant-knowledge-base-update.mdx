# Virtual Assistant Knowledge Base Update

A knowledge base quietly becomes unreliable when a policy changes in one place and remains old everywhere else. Updating it well is an editorial maintenance job with dependencies, evidence, and ownership. A virtual assistant can coordinate the change, compare versions, and surface gaps while the authority for the policy remains with the responsible business owner.

## Virtual Assistant Knowledge Base Updates: Find the authoritative change

Begin with change intake, not page editing. Capture what changed, why it changed, its effective date, the approved source, and the person authorized to confirm interpretation. A chat message, draft announcement, or remembered instruction can be a lead, but it is not automatically the source of truth. If two approved sources disagree, pause the edit and ask the owner which one governs.

Classify the change as policy, product, workflow, terminology, or contact information. The category helps locate likely dependents, but do not use it as proof that a page needs revision. Compare the current approved version with the proposed version line by line. Mark additions, removals, changed conditions, examples, screenshots, links, and words that alter who may act.

## Trace the dependency graph

Treat each page as a node connected to the places that quote, link to, summarize, or operationalize it. A policy page may feed a customer article, a staff procedure, an onboarding page, a form instruction, and a saved response. A change to one phrase can therefore create several different update tasks. Record the relationship and its confidence instead of relying on memory.

```text
Approved policy change
        |
        +> customer explanation -> search terms and links
        +> staff procedure -> training note and checklist
        +> form instruction -> field text and confirmation
        +> screenshot -> capture and accessibility text
        +> release note -> effective-date announcement
```

Impact mapping should include pages that do not use the same wording. Search for old terminology, examples, product names, and questions a reader would ask. Ask the source owner whether a dependent page is merely informative or whether its instructions could cause a different action.

## Assign review ownership by consequence

The editor who makes a page readable is not necessarily the person who can approve its meaning. Give each affected item an editing owner and an authority reviewer. A low-risk spelling correction may need only editorial review. A changed eligibility rule, safety instruction, financial commitment, or customer promise needs the relevant subject owner. If no reviewer is named, leave the item pending rather than publishing a guess.

Build the freshness-risk matrix before deciding the order of work.

Risk is the combination of likelihood and consequence, not the age of a page alone. A recently edited page can be risky if its source changed yesterday.

## Compare versions beyond visible prose

Review headings, links, tables, callouts, downloadable files, metadata, and alt text. Check whether a new term is searchable by the words customers still use. If a page's URL changes, create a redirect or a clearly approved replacement path. Do not leave a link that lands on an archived explanation when an active instruction exists.

Screenshots deserve special attention. A stale image can contradict accurate text and may be more persuasive to a reader than the paragraph beside it. Re-capture only from an approved environment, remove unnecessary private data, update the caption and alternative text, and check that the image is still legible on a small screen. The [FTC privacy and security guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security) offers a useful reminder to avoid exposing personal information in operational material.

## Work a change through an example

Imagine a business changes its account-recovery process: identity verification is now required before a reset link is issued, and the new rule takes effect on September 1. Intake records the approved policy page, effective date, reviewer, and old instruction. Impact mapping finds the customer help article, the internal support procedure, a saved response, a form hint, a screenshot, and the search terms “forgot password” and “reset access.”

The editor updates the direct instruction and marks the old version for archival treatment. The authority reviewer confirms that the exception for locked administrators remains separate. The support procedure receives the new verification step and an escalation route for failed checks. The saved response changes its wording but does not promise a time or outcome that the policy does not provide. The form hint points to the revised explanation. The screenshot is replaced because it shows the old reset screen. A release note states the effective date and links to the active article.

Before publication, a reviewer follows the dependency chain as a reader would. They search both the new and old phrases, open every related link, test the redirect, and confirm that the archived page is clearly marked. After publication, support staff report whether incoming questions still use an unaddressed phrase. That feedback can reveal a missing synonym or an explanation that is technically correct but hard to follow.

## Schedule effective dates and redirects

A future change should not silently replace today's instruction. Keep the effective date visible in the review record and, where confusion is likely, on the page itself. Coordinate the publishing window with the owner of the underlying change. If an old page must remain for historical reasons, label its status and route readers to the current instruction. If it should no longer be discoverable, use the approved redirect or retirement process and record the destination.

Release notes should say what readers need to know, not repeat every editorial action. Include the affected behavior, effective date, and link to the current source. Avoid announcing a change before its authority owner has approved the wording.

## Measure freshness through feedback

Post-publication checks should look for evidence of confusion: search queries that lead to outdated pages, repeated questions about the same step, broken links, screenshots that no longer match the interface, and support notes that quote retired language. Assign each signal to an owner and decide whether it means correction, clarification, synonym support, or a source-policy question.

Set review reminders based on risk and change signals, not an arbitrary promise that every page is equally current. A low-risk background article may need a periodic check. A page tied to an active product or policy should be reviewed when its source changes. Record the last authority review and the next trigger so freshness is explainable.

For complementary editing controls, see the [editorial quality checklist](/blog/virtual-assistant-editorial-quality-checklist) and [document review process](/blog/virtual-assistant-document-review-process). A dependable knowledge base is not one that never changes. It is one whose changes can be traced from an approved source to every reader-facing dependency.
