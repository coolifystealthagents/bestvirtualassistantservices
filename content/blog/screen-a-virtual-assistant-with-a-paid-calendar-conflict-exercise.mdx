# Screen a virtual assistant with a paid calendar conflict exercise

## How to approach: Screen a virtual assistant with a paid calendar conflict exercise

A calendar conflict exercise works when the task, the evidence, and the review standard are fixed before the interview. The candidate should receive a small fixture calendar, a short brief, and a clear time limit. The reviewer should score the decisions against a written rubric rather than an impression of confidence. Pay the candidate for the exercise and state the limit in the brief.

Choose three conflicts that require different judgments. One should be a routine overlap, one should involve a senior meeting that cannot move, and one should involve a requester who has not explained the priority. Add a fourth item that looks urgent but is already covered by a delegate. That mix shows whether the candidate reads context or simply moves the newest request.

## Build a safe calendar fixture

Replace real names, clients, and meeting links with invented but consistent data. Use a blank test calendar that the candidate can edit without touching production systems. Remove phone numbers, signatures, and any internal link that would expose staff or tools. Keep the thread structure realistic, including a forwarded note and a conflicting invitation.

Write a short key for the reviewer that explains which meetings are fixed, which can move, and which need a question. The candidate should see the same constraints except for the answers. If the exercise asks for an email reply, provide a draft-only mailbox. A fixture that depends on live access turns a skills test into a security risk.

## Score judgment, not calendar speed

Use a scorecard with four dimensions: accuracy, priority order, communication, and escalation. Accuracy covers names, times, time zones, and whether the candidate preserved the fixed meeting. Priority order asks the candidate to explain why one conflict came first. Communication covers the tone and clarity of the message to the requester.

Escalation judgment is the dimension buyers forget. A candidate who spots a senior meeting, an external commitment, or an unclear authority should stop and ask. Give credit for a well-written question when the brief is genuinely ambiguous. Penalize silent guessing more heavily than a slow but correct handoff.

## Compare candidates on the same evidence

Review the exercises in the same order and with the same reviewer, then record one sentence of evidence for each score. Do not let a strong interview answer overwrite a weak sample. Keep the candidate's written questions beside the output because they show how the person handles uncertainty. If two reviewers disagree, discuss the specific evidence rather than averaging the scores.

Ask each candidate to walk through one decision after the sample is submitted. Listen for whether the person can name the source, the rule, and the consequence. A candidate who says the meeting felt important has not explained the decision. A candidate who says the requester's contract renewal made the meeting fixed has given you something you can test again.

## Watch for risky shortcuts

A fast sample can be a warning sign when the candidate moves a fixed meeting, invents a priority, or marks a conflict resolved without telling the requester. Check whether the candidate used the approved template or quietly wrote a new one. Look for replies that copy the requester's wording without checking the calendar record. Those shortcuts often survive a trial period because nobody reads the sent folder closely.

Also check how the candidate handled the item that was already covered by a delegate. A reply that sounds helpful but duplicates the work is not a good outcome. The sample should make the boundary visible without turning the exercise into a trick. Tell candidates in the brief that some items may need a question rather than an action.

## Write the hiring recommendation

End the review with a short recommendation that names the evidence, the risk, and the next step. Separate what the candidate did well from what the buyer would need to coach. A recommendation that says good communicator is not useful without an example. A recommendation that says preserved the fixed meeting and asked one clear question is useful.

Keep the recommendation with the scorecard so a later reviewer can see how the decision was made. If the buyer hires the candidate, use the weakest score to shape the first month of coaching. If the buyer does not hire, record the specific gap so the next exercise can test it. The exercise is only worth the time if it changes a decision.
