# Evidence Standards for Virtual Assistant Content Briefs: A Research Brief

A content brief is useful when it makes the reader's question, evidence threshold, and editorial decision visible. It is weak when it only assembles keywords or promises an outcome.

## The brief should start with a decision

State what the reader must understand and what the owner must approve. Google describes helpful content in terms of people-first usefulness, while usability research shows that readers scan for answers before committing to a page [1][3]. Connect the brief to the [content research library](/research) and the site's [services overview](/services) without turning either into proof of a claim.

## Three evidence layers reduce ambiguity

Separate primary facts, contextual interpretation, and the proposed service implication. A government standard can establish a control. It cannot establish that one provider performs it well. Privacy, advertising, accessibility, and security guidance should each be cited for the specific claim they support [4][5][6][8].

## A review table keeps claims honest

| Layer | Brief question | Owner check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fact | What does the source actually say? | Link and date are recorded |
| Interpretation | What does it mean for this reader? | Assumptions are visible |
| Recommendation | What action follows? | Scope is approved |

Copyright and licensing constraints also belong in the brief when research or quotations will shape the page [7].

## Methodology and limitations

This review compares official guidance on helpful content, accessibility, privacy, advertising, security, copyright, and digital policy, checked on 2026-08-11. It evaluates briefing decisions, not ranking or lead outcomes.

## Key takeaways

- Give every material claim a source and a purpose.
- Label interpretation instead of presenting it as a fact.
- Keep the approving owner responsible for the final public promise.
