# How complete is the task handover record at the end of a shift?

## Research question and scope

This is a proposed research protocol, not a report of completed findings. It asks how often an end-of-shift handover record names the open work, the source, and the next action. The result would describe the quality of the handover rather than the quality of the assistant. Record the observation time so the next reviewer can tell when the evidence was checked.

The protocol does not measure whether the next assistant made a good decision. It measures whether the record supplied the evidence needed to make one. The sponsor should name the process decision the result could inform. A short note with four fields is easier to use than a long narrative.

## Population and unit of analysis

Define the population as all handover records created at the end of a shift during a stated window. The unit is one record, not one assistant or one client. Records for shifts with no open work should be included if the process requires a handover note. Write the deadline as a date and a local time.

Link each record to the tasks it references so the same work is not counted twice across shifts. A task that carries over three shifts appears in three records but remains one task in the task-level view. Test the rule on one real task before applying it to the queue. Review the first few notes together before the process expands.

## Definitions and measures

Define completeness as the presence of the open task, the source record, the next action, and the owner. Define clarity as whether a second reviewer can state the next action after reading the record. Measure the two separately because a complete record can still be unclear. If the source is unclear, route the question instead of guessing.

Report the share of records that meet each field and the share that meet all fields. A high field rate can hide a missing owner or a vague next action. The combination is what determines whether the handover is usable. Keep the original note beside the corrected version.

## Evidence standard

Link each record to the tasks and sources it mentions. A record that says follow up with the client is not evidence of a handover. A record that names the client, the open item, the last contact, and the next date is. Record the exception and the person who approved it.

Have a second reviewer read the record and write the next action without asking a question. Compare that answer with the original owner's intent. The comparison is the evidence of clarity, not the reviewer's general impression. Record the observation time so the next reviewer can tell when the evidence was checked.

## Sampling and collection

Use a census of the window if the volume is manageable. If sampling is necessary, stratify by shift, client, and task type. Select the sample before reviewing clarity and keep a log of replacements. If the source is unclear, route the question instead of guessing.

Pilot the review on a few records and compare the reviewers' answers. Clarify what counts as a source and what counts as a next action. Record the rule used when a record refers to a task that is already closed. Keep the boundary visible in the place where the work is assigned.

## Analysis and reporting

Report completeness and clarity by shift and by task type. Show the share of records that required a follow-up conversation before the next assistant could act. That follow-up rate is a practical measure of handover cost. Review the first few notes together before the process expands.

Present the results as a description of the records, not a judgment about the assistants. A weak record may reflect time pressure, a missing template, or an unclear process. Name the likely causes and the records needed to test them. Keep the evidence with the record rather than in a separate log.

## Limits and sources of error

The reviewer's ability to infer the next action may depend on familiarity with the client. A record that is clear to an experienced reviewer may be unclear to a new one. The study should state the reviewer's context. Keep the evidence with the record rather than in a separate log.

The sample may miss handovers that happened verbally and were never written down. Those cases are important because they represent the weakest evidence. Use a shift log or a task list to estimate the size of that gap. If the source is unclear, route the question instead of guessing.

## Review cadence

Repeat the review after a template change or a shift pattern change. Compare the same measures and the same task types. A second review can test whether the change reduced the follow-up rate. Keep the original note beside the corrected version.

Assign the follow-up to the team lead and set a date. The protocol should produce a specific decision about which fields the handover template must require. Ask whether a new reviewer could act on the record without a conversation. Record the exception and the person who approved it.
